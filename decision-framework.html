How REITs actually manage their assets

Understanding this is critical — it reveals both their strength (professionalism) and their weakness (investor powerlessness).

Asset management (strategic)
The fund manager (e.g., Jadwa Capital, Alkhabeer Capital) has exclusive authority over the fund. They decide:
  • Which properties to acquire or dispose of
  • Investment strategy and portfolio allocation
  • Leverage ratio (max 50% of total assets per CMA)
  • When to refinance or restructure holdings
  • Development projects (max 25% of fund)

Investors have zero say in buy/sell decisions.

Property management (operational)
CMA requires a licensed independent property manager — separate from the fund manager. They handle:
  • Day-to-day operations, maintenance, repairs
  • Tenant leasing, renewals, evictions
  • Rent collection and vacancy management
  • Budgeting and cost control per property
  • Staff hiring at individual properties

Investors never interact with property operations.

Valuation & oversight
Before buying or selling any asset, the fund manager must get two independent valuations from CMA-accredited appraisers. Quarterly reports must detail every property: physical description, financials, risk factors, and commercial terms. An independent custodian holds assets separate from the fund manager's own assets.

REITs give you liquidity and low fees. They take away all control, transparency, and personal connection to the asset. The question is: which matters more to Saudi investors?

02 — Key Questions

The hard questions we haven't answered

Q1: How do multiple micro-investors make decisions on a single asset?
The REIT model for comparison
REITs solve this cleanly: the fund manager has exclusive authority. Investors can't vote on buy/sell/manage decisions — only on firing the fund manager or changing fund terms. This works because REITs offer instant exit (don't like the decisions? sell your units). We don't have instant exit, so we need a different answer.
Options for us
A. Platform decides (centralized PM) — We appoint a Fal-licensed property manager. Investors receive monthly reports but don't vote on operations. Simplest. Most like REITs but with per-asset transparency.

B. Investor vote (DAO-like) — Major decisions (sell property, change PM, capital expenditure >X) go to investor vote. Democratic but slow. No legal precedent in Saudi law for fractional property governance.

C. Hybrid — Platform handles day-to-day. Major exit/sale decisions require 60%+ investor approval. Mirrors the REIT model but with more transparency and investor voice on the single decision that matters most: when to sell.

Recommendation: Option C. Copy the REIT model for operations (centralized PM, professional management) but add investor vote for the exit decision. This addresses the #1 fear (liquidity) by giving investors a voice in the only decision they truly care about. HissaTech and Ghanem both use centralized management.

Q2: Should we run the asset post-tokenization?
How REITs do it
CMA requires REITs to hire an independent property manager — they cannot self-manage. The PM is a licensed contractor separate from the fund manager, with their own fiduciary duties. This separation protects investors from conflicts of interest.
Options for us
A. Third-party PM via Fal-licensed company — 2% of rent, passed to investors. We stay asset-light, focused on the platform. Follows the REIT model. Clean separation.

B. In-house PM — Higher margin (keep the 2%) but requires Fal licensing, hiring, operational complexity. Conflict of interest risk. Distraction from platform.

C. Developer retains PM — Cheapest. But: developer already wanted to exit. Misaligned incentives. Trust problem.

Recommendation: Option A. Follow the REIT playbook: independent, licensed third-party PM. Pass cost through transparently. CMA requires this for REITs for good reason — it protects investors. We should adopt the same standard voluntarily. In-house PM is Phase 3+ (500+ units).

Q3: How do investors exit? (The hardest question)
Why this is our weakest point
REITs win on liquidity — sell on Tadawul in seconds. We have no secondary market pre-tokenization. An investor who wants out has no clear path. This is the single biggest investor objection and our most honest weakness.
Exit solutions (ranked by feasibility)
1. Fixed-term hold with scheduled exit — 3-year hold. At year 3, property is sold or re-offered. Clear, predictable. HissaTech uses 3-year contracts. Investors know what they're getting into.

2. Annual property sale vote — Each year, investors vote: hold or sell? If 60%+ says sell, property goes to market. Gives investors a voice without promising instant liquidity.

3. Platform matching (bulletin board) — Investor A sells fraction to Investor B. We facilitate. Low tech. Requires buyer demand to exist.

4. Platform buyback reserve — Set aside 10% of placement fees as buyback fund. Buy back fractions at 90% NAV. Capital intensive. Risky.

5. REGA tokenization (long-term) — Once RER tokens live, secondary market on blockchain. Timeline unknown. Can't promise this.

Recommendation: Combine #1 and #2. Launch with 3-year fixed term + annual vote. Add bulletin-board matching in Year 2. Be radically transparent: "This is not liquid. If you need instant exit, buy a REIT." Honesty is the only viable strategy here. The REGA secondary market is the long-term answer but cannot be promised.
